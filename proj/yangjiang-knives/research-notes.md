## The big one: the premise flips

Bryan's starting idea: "China were the OG knifelords, Germany and Japan took the title." The sources say China
never held the title as an exporter:
- **Exporting was illegal.** The Qing Code filed iron goods and blades under military law: 100 strokes of the heavy
  bamboo for exporting ironwares, strangulation for weapons. The Canton System (1757–1842) let a crewman leave with one
  wok and one axe (q1).
- **China was the customer.** Ming China imported 100,000+ Japanese swords through the tally trade, roughly
  1404–mid-1500s; q2 says 128,000+.
- **Yangjiang is young.** The "1,400 years" is an invented tradition. It traces to one Qing-era clan genealogy about
  one saber in one tomb (646 CE). The documented trade starts on Blacksmith Lane (打铁巷) in 1862–74 (q1).

So the story isn't "lost and reclaimed." It's **"never allowed in the game, and now it owns the board."** That's a
better story, and it still answers Bryan's question. The "best knife country" title really did change hands, but
between other countries:
- **Sheffield → Solingen** (late 1800s, q2)
- **Japan: cheap → prestige** (1980s on, q2)

## Emergent narratives (candidates for the spine)
1. **Laws make knife capitals.** Each center rose or fell on a law:
   - Qing export ban (China kept out)
   - Sakai Kiwame, the shogun's seal (Sakai protected)
   - 1876 Haitōrei sword ban (Seki forced into kitchen knives)
   - Indian Arms Act 1878 (India's wootz industry killed)
   - Solingen name law 1938, 1994 decree (Solingen protected)
   - US tariffs 2026 (China squeezed)
2. **Swords into kitchen knives.** The dark joke, sourced:
   - Solingen's guilds were sword guilds and turned to cutlery as guns replaced swords.
   - Seki's swordsmiths turned to kitchen knives because the 1876 ban killed their trade.
   - The Solingen name law was passed on July 25, 1938 and printed in the Reichsgesetzblatt. That's the Nazi-era law
     gazette.
3. **Everyone's heritage is partly marketing.**
   - Yangjiang's Lady Xian saber.
   - Bryan's line, "samurai are fake though fuck those wannabes", is the right instinct. Shun and Miyabi sell
     samurai lore on factory knives. Miyabi is owned by Zwilling, a German company (q2).
   - Fake-Japanese Yangjiang brands sell the same lore with nothing behind it.
   - The arrow reverses: Ming China bought real Japanese swords; today Yangjiang makes fake "Japanese" knives.
4. **Same technique, different prestige.**
   - Yangjiang's *xiagang* (夹钢): a hard core between soft iron cheeks, often from scrap vehicle springs (q1).
   - Japan's *san-mai* / *kasumi*: the same sandwich (q2, q4). One is sold as a craft, the other was a poor man's
     workaround.
5. **Copy first, then lead.**
   - The gyuto is Japan's adaptation of the Western chef's knife (q4).
   - Fake "Solingen" stamps were common enough to need a law (q2).
   - China's 10Cr15CoMoV is a VG-10 match, and 5Cr15MoV matches the German X50CrMoV15 (q4).
6. **The capital absorbs the heritage brands.** Wangmazi (Beijing, founded 1651) and Zhang Xiaoquan (Hangzhou,
   400 years) both moved or expanded production into Yangjiang (q3a, q3b).
7. **The answer to "does the country matter?" is no:** geometry and heat treatment matter (q4).
   - **Geometry:** thickness behind the edge matters more than steel. America's Test Kitchen's top pick was a
     budget Victorinox over forged German knives.
   - **Heat treatment:** a good heat treatment beats fancy steel. Badly treated VG-10 loses to well-treated
     8Cr13MoV.
   - **Same steel, different maker:** 10Cr15CoMoV matches VG-10 when heat-treated well (ChefPanko).
   - **Same factory town, real proof:** Cangshan's Yangjiang-made 14C28N beat its own German-steel line on CATRA
     tests.
   - **Damascus pattern:** decorative only.

## Primer moments that tie to the history (good interactives)
- **The Wenwudao (文武刀, "civil-military knife"),** 1920s, He Quanli. A thin slicing front and a thick chopping heel
  on one blade. It's the edge-angle trade-off in a single object, and the history and primer meet here.
- **The garlic smash (拍蒜断刀), 2022.** A Zhang Xiaoquan cleaver snapped smashing garlic. 60,000+ sold, 11
  complaints (q3a). That's hardness vs toughness, shown with the most Chinese kitchen move there is.
- **Single vs double bevel:** steering, the hollow back (*urasuki*), and why Japan's raw-fish cuisine wanted it.
- **Angle vs edge life:** CATRA numbers (q4).
  - 20° inclusive: about 800 mm of card cut
  - 34°: about 450 mm
  - 50°: about 250 mm
- **Check your knife:** "Solingen" is legally protected; "German steel", "Designed in Germany" and "Japanese steel
  core" are not (q4).
