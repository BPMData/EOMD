# **The Industrial Evolution of Yangjiang Cutlery: Metallurgical Traditions, Regional Political Economy, and the Making of China's Blade Capital (Origins to 1990\)**

## **Historiography and the Myth of Antiquity: Deconstructing the "1,400-Year" Claim**

Modern marketing campaigns, commercial brand literature, and regional tourism brochures frequently assert that cutlery manufacturing in Yangjiang (阳江), Guangdong Province, possesses an unbroken institutional pedigree extending back more than 1,400 years1. A rigorous examination of regional historiography, lineage records, and metallurgical archaeology demonstrates that this figure is an invented tradition constructed in the late 20th century to endow a modern export-oriented light-industrial cluster with ancient cultural prestige1.  
The promotional narrative traces the craft's inception to 557 CE (the first year of the Yongding era under the Chen Dynasty), when the indigenous Xian chieftain, Lady Xian (冼夫人), stationed military garrisons throughout the Liangyang (两阳, denoting modern Yangjiang and Yangchun) region1. The documentary anchor for this claim appears in a single lineage compilation: the Qing-era *Feng Clan Genealogy* (清《冯氏族谱》)1. The text states:  
"……冯大将军生平所用八十二角鹅眉宣锦大刀纳于冢中"  
*"The eighty-two-angled Emei Xuanjin saber used by General Feng during his lifetime was deposited within his tomb."*1  
"General Feng" refers to Lady Xian’s grandson, Feng Ang (冯盎), who served as the Magistrate of Songkang (宋康令, encompassing modern Yangxi) under the Sui and Tang dynasties, died in 646 CE, and was buried at Beishan in Yangjiang1. Local commercial lore identifies this mortuary saber as the *dashadao* (大杀刀, "great battle blade") and maintains that the military smiths who accompanied Lady Xian and Feng Ang settled permanently along the Moyang River, transferring military blade-forging techniques to local civilian smiths and initiating the city's cutlery industry1.  
A critical historiographical and metallurgical audit undermines this narrative on three structural grounds:  
First, the *Feng Clan Genealogy* is a late-imperial lineage document compiled during the Qing dynasty, over a millennium after Feng Ang’s death. Official dynastic records of the period, including the *Book of Sui* (*Sui Shu*) and the *Old Book of Tang* (*Jiu Tang Shu*), contain no corroborating references to an armory complex, metallurgical guild, or specialized civilian blade sector within the Moyang River basin during the 6th or 7th centuries.  
Second, the ritual deposition of an elite martial saber within an aristocratic chieftain’s tomb reflects high-status mortuary customs rather than an underlying civilian manufacturing economy1. The presence of an imported or custom-forged prestige weapon in a burial context provides no evidence of local workshops or continuous toolmaking.  
Third, the archaeological record of western Guangdong lacks evidence of early metallurgical industrialization. Extensive archaeological surveys across Yangjiang and Yangchun have identified Paleolithic cave sites such as Dushizai and extensive Tang and Song ceramic kiln complexes, including the Shiwan and Namo kiln sites producing export-grade trade ceramics6, yet they have revealed no iron-smelting blast furnaces, puddling hearths, or weaponsmith workshops dating to the 6th through 14th centuries6.  
The excavation of the *Nanhai I* shipwreck, a Southern Song merchant vessel discovered in the waters off Yangjiang, yielded substantial tonnages of cast-iron pots, iron bars, and iron nails7. Metallurgical analysis, however, traces these trade commodities to the major iron-smelting centers of the Pearl River Delta (Foshan) and Minnan (Fujian), rather than to indigenous Yangjiang workshops12.  
The earliest verifiable primary documentation of commercial cutlery production in Yangjiang dates to the late Qing dynasty. As recorded in the Republican-era *Yangjiang County Gazetteer* (*Minguo Shisi Nian Yangjiang Zhi* / 民国十四年《阳江志》, published in 1925\) and municipal records, civilian blade-smithing coalesced during the Tongzhi reign (1862–1874) in the old walled city along what became known as Datie Xiang (打铁巷, "Blacksmith Lane")1. The gazetteer notes: "铜器·小刀花剪俱佳，货行远近" (*"Copperwares, pocketknives, and pattern shears are all of fine quality; these goods circulate near and far."*)2. Reliable documentary and material evidence confirms that Yangjiang's commercial cutlery history spans roughly 150 to 180 years, rather than fourteen centuries.

## **Comparative Metallurgy: Yangjiang’s Utilitarian Cutlery vs. China’s Elite Blade Traditions**

To locate Yangjiang within China's broader metallurgical history, its vernacular craft methods must be distinguished from imperial state metallurgy and elite artistic sword-making.

| Dimension | Classical Imperial Metallurgy (Han–Song) | Longquan Sword Tradition (Zhejiang) | Yangjiang Cutlery Sector (Guangdong) |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Primary Functional Focus** | Military armor, spears, martial ring-pommel *dao*, and agricultural implements15. | Prestigious double-edged swords (*jian*), Daoist liturgical blades, and scholar-gentry status symbols. | Domestic kitchen cleavers (*caidao*), tailoring scissors, pocketknives, and agricultural sickles1. |
| **Smelting & Materials** | Smelting from raw iron ore via blast furnaces; liquid pig iron, *chao gang* decarburization, and *guangang* co-fusion7. | Refined billet forging, traditional folded steel (*huawengang*), and selected regional crucible steels. | Secondary forge-finishing; recycling of domestic scrap, imported railroad track, and automotive leaf springs1. |
| **Lamination Structure** | Layered billets, complex composite forgings, and differential hardening (*bailiangang*)15. | Folded pattern-welding (*sanmai* or insert construction), clay-tempered differential martensitic hardening. | *Xiagang* (sandwich lamination inserting a recycled high-carbon core between mild iron cheeks)1. |
| **Socio-Economic Base** | Imperial state monopolies (*Tie Guan*) and bureaucratic military arsenals15. | Aristocratic, imperial, military, and Daoist gentry patronage. | Agrarian household by-employment, municipal blacksmith guilds, and export-driven light industry13. |

Imperial Chinese metallurgy achieved significant early breakthroughs. By the Warring States period and the Western Han Dynasty, state-managed iron monopolies operated high-temperature blast furnaces powered by double-acting piston bellows, producing liquid pig iron (生铁)7. To overcome the structural brittleness of unrefined cast iron, Han metallurgists developed cast-iron decarburization and puddling (*chao gang* / 炒钢), stirring molten pig iron in an oxidizing atmosphere to burn away excess carbon and convert it into malleable wrought iron or steel7.  
Artisans refined this material through "hundred-refinements steel" (*bailiangang* / 百炼钢), repeatedly folding, heating, and hammering billets to extrude silicate slag inclusions and distribute carbon evenly, producing high-performance laminated blades15. By the 6th century, the metallurgist Qiwu Huaiwen codified co-fusion steelmaking (*guangang* / 灌钢), melting high-carbon cast iron over low-carbon wrought iron to produce homogeneous steel7.  
Elite swordsmithing centers, exemplified by Longquan in Zhejiang Province, operated within this technical tradition. Tracing its roots to the legendary Spring and Autumn swordsmith Ou Yezi, Longquan flourished through the Song, Ming, and Qing dynasties by catering to military officers, imperial courts, Daoist liturgical practitioners, and the scholar-gentry. Longquan swords (*jian*) were prestige weapons produced through intricate pattern welding (*huawengang* / 花纹钢), selective clay-tempered heat treatment (*futu cuihuo* / 覆土淬火), and elaborate acid etching, producing blades valued for both aesthetic appeal and martial performance.  
Yangjiang occupied a fundamentally different industrial niche. It was never an imperial arsenal or a center of luxury ceremonial weapons. Instead, its artisans operated within a vernacular, utilitarian cutlery tradition focused on domestic items: kitchen cleavers (*caidao* 菜刀), tailor's shears, pocketknives, and agricultural sickles1.  
Yangjiang lacked primary blast furnace operations that smelted raw iron ore into pig iron. Artisans were secondary forge-finishers who purchased merchant bar iron or reprocessed scrap iron1. They relied on forge-lamination techniques known as *xiagang* (夹钢) or *tiebao gang* (铁包钢): rather than fashioning homogenous blades from costly refined steel, blacksmiths forge-welded a thin core of high-carbon steel—frequently extracted from discarded vehicle springs—between two cheeks of soft, shock-absorbing wrought iron or mild steel1.  
This composite construction minimized the consumption of scarce high-carbon steel while ensuring the blade maintained a razor-sharp cutting edge supported by durable iron cheeks that could be resharpened on household stones. Quenching operations used simple river water, mineral slurries, or vegetable oils, optimizing production for functional durability and low cost rather than aesthetic refinement.

## **The Political Economy of Non-Export in Pre-Modern China**

A recurring question in global economic history is why pre-modern China, despite its early metallurgical advancements, never established an international blade and cutlery export industry comparable to Solingen in Germany, Sheffield in England, or Sakai in Japan, even while dominating international maritime commerce in silk, porcelain, and tea.  
A common explanation is that China’s immense domestic market absorbed its entire metallurgical output. While China’s demographic expansion—from roughly 100 million in the Northern Song to more than 300 million by the mid-Qing—demanded massive quantities of plowshares, cooking woks, and kitchen cleavers15, domestic demand alone does not fully explain this absence. Domestic demand for silk, porcelain, and tea was also enormous, yet all three became leading global export commodities. The absence of an early Chinese blade export industry resulted from strategic legal prohibitions, imperial trade systems, and the economic geography of early modern freight.  
Imperial dynasties classified iron tools and weapons as dual-use goods with direct military applications7. From the Han Dynasty's embargoes against the Xiongnu and the southern state of Nanyue to the maritime bans of the Ming and Qing, imperial administrations feared that iron exports would equip nomadic adversaries or coastal pirates15.  
Both the *Ming Code* (*Da Ming Lü*) and the *Qing Code* (*Da Qing Lü Li*) treated the cross-border sale of ferrous goods under military law (*Bing Lü·Guanjin* 兵律·关津, "Statutes on Frontier Passes and Coastal Harbors")19. The statutes stated that anyone who illicitly transported horses, military equipment, iron goods (*tiehuo*), copper cash, silks, or satins across land borders or overseas for sale was subject to 100 strokes of the heavy bamboo, while those caught exporting finished weapons faced execution by strangulation19.  
Specific Qing sub-statutes (*tiaoli*) penalized the illicit export of scrap iron and manufactured ironwares exceeding 100 catties (approximately 60 kilograms) with confiscation of cargo, flogging, and penal servitude22. These statutes were strictly enforced during the 17th-century maritime conflicts against the Taiwan-based Zheng regime and were renewed in the 18th and early 19th centuries to suppress piracy along the Guangdong and Fujian coasts19.  
The Canton System (一口通商), which governed Western maritime trade from 1757 to 1842, further restricted metal exports19. By confining Western merchantmen to the port of Guangzhou and requiring all transactions to pass through the licensed Cohong monopoly (*Shisanhang* 十三行), the Qing state exercised tight control over export cargoes19.  
Foreign traders were barred from visiting interior production centers or purchasing goods outside official channels24. Port regulations restricted outgoing vessels to a minimal inventory of ironwares: a single cast-iron cooking wok and one utility axe per crewman, while excess blades or bulk metals were seized as contraband31. The imperial customs administration focused foreign trade on commodities that generated predictable customs revenues without posing security risks: black and green teas, woven silks, and porcelain19.

| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |

Transport economics and industrial energy sources also shaped these trade patterns. During the age of sail, maritime freight was constrained by vessel capacity. High-value goods like silk and tea absorbed transportation costs and risks, whereas carbon-steel cutlery was low-margin, heavy, and vulnerable to rust from maritime moisture.  
Furthermore, while European cutlery centers like Solingen and Sheffield industrialized during the 18th and early 19th centuries by utilizing water-powered grindstones, coal-fired crucible steel, and steam-driven trip hammers, China’s southern iron trade faced charcoal shortages due to regional deforestation. This raised smelting costs and left domestic knife-making dependent on manual anvil forging in small workshops13, rendering Chinese cutlery uncompetitive on the global market until modern industrialization in the late 20th century.

## **The Historical Trajectory of Yangjiang Cutlery: Late Qing to 1990**

### **The Late Qing Crucible: Local Geography and Datie Xiang**

Yangjiang’s development into a regional cutlery center accelerated during the mid-to-late 19th century, shaped by geography, resource availability, and local economic conditions:  
The Moyang River basin provided regional logistical advantages. Alluvial iron sand (铁砂) deposits were panned along local river shoals, while the upstream forests of Yangchun supplied charcoal for small forges1. River craft carried scrap cast iron, agricultural tools, and fuels downriver directly into Yangjiang's urban core1.  
Yangjiang’s position along the South China Sea created consistent demand for edged tools from the fishing fleet and coastal transport junks, which required rigging knives, fish-gutting blades, adzes, and heavy rope shears1. Inland, farming communities created a steady market for sickles, grass knives, and cleavers1.  
Economic pressures in the local agrarian economy also encouraged craft specialization. Rugged topography, poor soils, and population growth along the coast limited agricultural yields, prompting rural families to take up metalworking as secondary employment. Blacksmiths emerged in surrounding villages such as Naluo (那洛村) and migrated into the county seat1.  
During the Tongzhi reign (1862–1874), these artisans concentrated in the western district of the county seat, forming Datie Xiang (打铁巷, "Blacksmith Lane")1. Over a dozen family workshops operated in this alley, forging kitchen cleavers, shears, and utility blades for itinerant traders across western Guangdong and eastern Guangxi2.

### **Interwar Innovations: The "Wenwudao" and the "Jifu Pocketknife"**

During the Republican era (1912–1949), craft innovations helped build Yangjiang’s reputation for cutlery beyond the immediate region:  
In the 1920s, village blacksmith He Quanli (何全利) established a workshop at Fanwutang along Twelfth Street in Yangjiang1. Traditional Cantonese cooking had required chefs to alternate between a delicate, thin slicing knife (*piandao* 片刀) and a thick, heavy cleaver for cutting bone (*zhandao* 斩刀).  
He Quanli forged a versatile composite cleaver known as the *Wenwudao* (文武刀, "Civil-Military Knife")1. By utilizing differential lamination and varying edge profiles, He ground the front two-thirds of the blade into a thin, acute slicing edge (the "Civil" section) and left the rear third near the heel thick and obtuse (the "Military" section)1. This design allowed a cook to slice meats and chop poultry bones with a single tool1. Selling for four *hao* of silver (4毫白银), the *Wenwudao* became a standard tool in restaurant kitchens throughout Guangzhou and Hong Kong1.  
In the 1930s, artisan Liang Jifu (梁季芙) introduced innovations in pocket cutlery13. Liang originally worked as a jeweler, fabricating decorative silver and gold accents for traditional bamboo tobacco pipes1. Carving dense bamboo knuckles required small, hard blades that held an edge without chipping1.  
Recognizing commercial opportunities in the market, Liang shifted his workshop to producing folding utility knives13. He abandoned soft local wrought iron in favor of high-carbon spring steel (弹簧钢) salvaged from discarded vehicle chassis and railway scrap1. By applying specialized oil-and-water quenching techniques, he achieved an edge that could shave hard bamboo knots without rolling or cracking1.  
Liang fitted these blades into handles made from polished water buffalo horn pinned to folded brass liners, stamping his personal mark ("季芙", *Jifu*) onto each ricasso1. His folding knives gained wide popularity, giving rise to the local saying: *"Three knives a day, covers daily bread"* (一日三把刀，吃用够花销)1.  
By 1949, according to the *Yangjiang County Gazetteer*, Yangjiang supported more than 300 independent knife-making artisans producing roughly 600,000 knives per year, establishing the artisanal base that would later support industrialization1.

### **Socialist Collectivization and Industrialization (1949–1978)**

Following the establishment of the People's Republic of China, the state reorganized Yangjiang's decentralized craft workshops into coordinated industrial units34. Between 1954 and 1956, private smithies were organized into four handicraft production cooperatives. In 1956, municipal authorities brought master smith Liang Jifu into the cooperative system to train apprentices and standardize heat-treatment methods across workshops35.  
In 1958, the state merged these four cooperatives into the unified State-Owned Yangjiang County Knife Factory (阳江县地方国营小刀厂, briefly named the State-Owned Jifu Knife Factory in the early 1960s)13. The factory transitioned from manual hammer forging to mechanized stamping presses, electric belt grinders, automated drop hammers, and centralized salt-bath heat-treatment systems13.  
By the late 1970s, the plant expanded its production from 20 traditional handcrafted patterns to over 280 standardized designs, producing multi-blade utility knives, stainless pocketknives, and export-grade kitchen cleavers13. The state enterprise served as an industrial incubator, training the machinists, die-makers, metallurgists, and managers who would go on to build the private cutlery industry under market reforms13.

### **Reform and Opening-Up: Li Lianghui and the Emergence of Shibazi (1983–1990)**

The economic reforms initiated under Deng Xiaoping in 1978 allowed private manufacturing to re-emerge in Yangjiang. In 1983, Li Lianghui (李良辉)—a master bladesmith trained in the Liang Jifu tradition who had worked for decades in the State-Owned Knife Factory—left the enterprise with seven co-workers36. Working from a temporary shed, they opened an independent blade forge37.  
Li Lianghui focused on optimizing carbon-steel forging for professional kitchen cleavers, while his son, Li Jihui (李积回), directed marketing and commercial growth32. Deriving a brand name from the Chinese character for their family surname—Li (李), which breaks down into "Ten" (十), "Eight" (八), and "Son" (子)—they founded **Shibazi** (阳江十八子)32.  
Throughout the 1980s, Shibazi led technical and structural changes in Yangjiang's private cutlery trade41. The enterprise shifted from manual finishing to mechanical rolling, built internal testing labs, and developed composite multi-layer steel plates40. In 1993, Shibazi produced China’s first all-steel, integral-handle kitchen cleaver (连体直出菜刀), which replaced the traditional pinned wooden handle that was vulnerable to rot and fracture, setting a new structural standard for Chinese commercial kitchen cutlery36.  
Other private firms and township-village enterprises (TVEs) grew alongside Shibazi during this period. Companies like Wingkwon (广东永光刀剪集团, founded by the Zhang family, tracing roots through local factories to 1928\) and Silver Eagle (银鹰) adopted automated stamping, cold-rolled stainless coil processing, and plastic injection molding44. These investments enabled local firms to secure OEM (Original Equipment Manufacturer) and ODM contracts with international brands, turning Yangjiang from a regional domestic supplier into a major export production center44.

## **Institutional Consecration: The "Knife and Scissors Capital of China" Title**

Yangjiang’s position as China's leading cutlery center was formally recognized in 2001 through national industrial accreditation46.  
The title **"Knife and Scissors Capital of China" (中国刀剪之都)** was officially conferred upon Yangjiang in **2001**46 jointly by two national governing bodies:

> 1. **The China Productivity Promotion Center Association** (中国生产力促进中心协会)32.  
> 2. **The National Daily Hardware Industry Productivity Promotion Center** (国家日用五金行业生产力促进中心)32.

The award reflected Yangjiang's high concentration of manufacturing facilities and export dominance:

* By 2001, the city housed over 1,000 cutlery and hardware manufacturing enterprises, producing **more than 60%** of China's total domestic knife and scissors output47.  
* Yangjiang-produced blades accounted for **over 80%** of China's cutlery exports, serving markets in more than 100 countries across North America, Europe, and Asia47.

Following this municipal designation, national trade bodies awarded specialized manufacturing titles to leading enterprises in the city. Shibazi was designated the **"China Kitchen Knife Center"** (中国菜刀中心)2, while Wingkwon was named the **"China Scissors Center"** (中国剪刀中心)2.  
In June 2002, the municipal government and national trade associations hosted the inaugural **China (Yangjiang) International Hardware Knives & Scissors Fair** (中国（阳江）国际五金刀剪博览会, the *Dao Bo Hui*), institutionalizing Yangjiang's role as a major production and supply hub for global consumer cutlery brands1.

## **Synthesis and Industrial Conclusions**

Yangjiang's development from a late-Qing blacksmithing street into China's foremost cutlery manufacturing center was shaped by specific economic transitions, adaptive uses of scrap materials, and shifting state policies, rather than an ancient 1,400-year metallurgical legacy:  
The popular narrative linking the city's cutlery industry to Lady Xian's military encampments in 557 CE relies on the Qing-dynasty *Feng Clan Genealogy*, which records an elite burial saber rather than a commercial blade guild1. The documentary and archaeological record shows that Yangjiang's commercial cutlery trade actually originated during the Tongzhi era (1862–1874) in Datie Xiang1.  
Pre-modern China’s absence from global blade export markets was not caused solely by domestic demand consuming supply. Instead, it was enforced by strategic legal prohibitions under the *Da Qing Lü Li*, which treated ironwares (*tiehuo*) and edged tools as contraband to prevent coastal raiders and regional rivals from obtaining weapons19.  
Enforced through the Canton System, these trade controls steered foreign commerce toward non-strategic luxury commodities like silk, tea, and porcelain19. Additionally, the rising cost of charcoal fuel in southern China and the high shipping weight of ferrous tools relative to their value kept traditional workshops focused on domestic markets until modern industrialization13.  
Yangjiang's emergence as an industrial cutlery cluster rested on practical economic and structural factors:

> 1. Artisans adapted to local raw material constraints by forge-laminating recycled vehicle spring steels and industrial scrap between cheeks of mild iron (*xiagang*)1.  
> 2. Blacksmiths developed designs closely tied to regional market needs, including He Quanli's *Wenwudao* for Cantonese cuisine and Liang Jifu's utility pocketknives1.  
> 3. The establishment of the State-Owned Yangjiang County Knife Factory in 1958 shifted production from manual anvils to mechanized presses, creating the technical foundation and trained workforce that later fueled private enterprise13.  
> 4. During the Reform and Opening-Up era, private entrepreneurs led by Li Lianghui and Li Jihui of Shibazi adopted automated tooling, full-chain production, and structural innovations like the integral-handle cleaver32.

When the China Productivity Promotion Center Association and the National Daily Hardware Industry Productivity Promotion Center designated Yangjiang the "Knife and Scissors Capital of China" in 2001, the award recognized the city's modern industrial concentration32. Producing more than 60% of domestic cutlery and generating over 80% of national blade exports, Yangjiang established its leadership through modern mechanization, flexible private entrepreneurship, and integration into global consumer supply chains47.
