Knife Country (DRAFT title)

One city on the south coast of China, Yangjiang, makes something like three quarters of the country’s knives and scissors, or so the city likes to say;[[yj-share]]

China, in turn, ships more than half of all the knives the world exports, counted in dollars;[[oec-2024]]

and yet under the Qing, loading Chinese iron goods onto a foreign ship could earn you a hundred blows with the heavy bamboo, and loading weapons could get you strangled.[[qing-code]]

So this isn’t quite the story people usually tell, about an ancient knife empire that lost its crown to the Germans and the Japanese and is now clawing it back; it’s stranger than that: China was barred from competing for the title in the first place, and the cities that did hold it won it the way everyone wins it, through laws, labels and the occasional outright lie, which is why the whole thing is told here through the marks stamped on the steel.

Keep an eye on the corner of the screen, too: when somebody wins the title of “the country that makes the good knives,” a belt shows up there, and it moves whenever the world changes its mind.

1 · The 1,400-year story

The brochures say fourteen hundred years; the paperwork says about a hundred and sixty.

Yangjiang likes to date its blades to the sixth century, and the whole of that claim rests on one line in a clan genealogy compiled under the Qing, more than a thousand years after the fact, which records that when General Feng Ang died in 646 his saber went into the tomb with him:[[feng-saber]] one sword, in one grave, with no forge, no workshop and no trade anywhere in sight.

The trade you can actually document starts in the 1860s, on a street just inside the old county town’s west gate that came to be called Blacksmith Lane, 打铁巷, where a dozen or so family forges hammered out cleavers, shears and pocketknives for the peddlers who worked western Guangdong;[[datie-xiang]] and by 1925 the county gazetteer could note, with some pride, that the town’s pocketknives and patterned shears were “all fine,” and that “the goods travel near and far”: 小刀花剪俱佳，货行远近.[[gazetteer-1925]]

兵律 · 关津: “Military Statutes: Frontier Passes and Harbours,” the section of the Qing code that covered iron goods.[[qing-code]]

2 · The ban

China wasn’t knocked off the throne; it was never let into the building, and the law that kept it out was written in Beijing.

The Qing code filed knives where you’d file anything that might end up in a pirate’s hand: under military law, in the statutes on frontier passes and harbours, which promised a hundred strokes of the heavy bamboo for running iron goods out of the country and strangulation for exporting weapons.[[qing-code]]

When Western ships were later confined to Canton, a departing crew could take one cast-iron wok and one axe per man, and anything more went to the customs house;[[canton-wok]] so silk, tea and porcelain went out by the shipload, and the cleaver stayed home.

Meanwhile the trade in good blades ran the other way: from 1404 into the middle of the 1500s, Ming China imported more than a hundred thousand Japanese swords through the official tally trade,[[ming-swords]] which is worth remembering for later, when the arrow turns round.

3 · Sheffield

The first city to hold the title was an English one, and it won it on the strength of better steel.

In the 1740s Benjamin Huntsman worked out, near Sheffield, how to melt steel in clay crucibles, which gave the town’s cutlers an even, slag-free metal that took an edge and held it better than anything their rivals could forge; the Company of Cutlers in Hallamshire, chartered by Parliament back in 1624, policed the marks, and for the better part of two centuries “Sheffield” on a blade was about the closest thing the world had to a guarantee.[[huntsman]]

That’s the belt, by the way, down in the corner: it’s Sheffield’s, for now. Before it moves, though, it’s worth knowing what “better steel” actually means.

More carbon: a harder edge, but a more brittle one, too.

Steel is iron with a little carbon in it, and in a knife that little carbon is most of the story; pick any of four real kitchen steels, from a cheap cleaver steel to a Japanese carbon steel, and drop the same thin edge onto a bone to see what each one does when it meets something hard.[[steels]][[hard-tough]]

4 · Made in Germany

Britain tried to shame German knives with a label; the label ended up selling them.

In 1876 Germany’s own commissioner at the Philadelphia exhibition, Franz Reuleaux, summed up his country’s goods in two words, billig und schlecht, cheap and bad;[[reuleaux]] but Solingen’s makers had taken to drop-forging and die-stamping while Sheffield still ran on a warren of small outworkers’ shops, and the German knives came in at a fifth to two-fifths cheaper.[[solingen-cheap]]

Sheffield’s answer was the Merchandise Marks Act of 1887, which made foreign goods say where they came from so that British shoppers would know what they were buying; they did know, as it turned out, and within twenty-odd years “Made in Germany” had become a mark people went looking for.[[mma-1887]] Then, in 1890, the McKinley Tariff all but shut Sheffield out of America, its biggest market,[[mckinley]] and the long slide in the chart began.

The belt, you’ll notice, has packed its bags for Solingen.
