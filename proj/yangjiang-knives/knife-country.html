It also, if you ask it, has fourteen hundred years of blade-making behind it, going back to the garrisons that Lady Xian, 冼夫人, stationed in the region in 557, and the military smiths who supposedly settled down beside the Moyang River and taught the locals their trade.6

But a great deal of that claim to knife-making aristocracy hangs on a single note, in a single text, about a single sword; and the real story turns out to be stranger, and to start a lot further back, because, much as Carl Sagan said that if you wish to make an apple pie from scratch you must first invent the universe,7 if you want the truth about Yangjiang and its claim to be the world’s foremost City of Knives, you must first invent the Qing dynasty.

What was the Qing dynasty?

China’s last imperial dynasty, 1644 to 1912, ruled by the Manchus from Beijing; it’s the empire the Opium Wars were fought against, and the one that fell in the revolution of 1911–12.8

Keep an eye on the bottom corner of the screen as you go: that belt belongs to whichever city the world considers its foremost City of Knives, and right now, before anyone has claimed it, it’s up for grabs. Tap it any time for the list of who’s held it.

1The 1,400-year story

The brochures say fourteen hundred years; the paperwork, once you go looking for it, says about a hundred and sixty.

Yangjiang likes to date its blades to the sixth century, and the whole of that claim rests on one line in a clan genealogy compiled under the Qing, more than a thousand years after the fact, which records that when General Feng Ang died in 646 his saber went into the tomb with him:9 one sword, in one grave, with no forge, no workshop and no trade anywhere in sight.

The trade you can actually document starts in the 1860s, on a street just inside the old county town’s west gate that came to be called Blacksmith Lane, 打铁巷, where a dozen or so family forges hammered out cleavers, shears and pocketknives for the peddlers who worked western Guangdong;10 and by 1925 the county gazetteer could note, with some pride, that the town’s pocketknives and patterned shears were “all fine,” and that “the goods travel near and far”: 小刀花剪俱佳，货行远近.11

兵律 · 关津: “Military Statutes: Frontier Passes and Harbours,” the section of the Qing code that covered iron goods.12

2The ban

China wasn’t knocked off the throne; it was never let into the building, and the law that kept it out was written in Beijing.

Which is odd, when you think about what China could do with iron. Two thousand years ago, under the Han, state ironworks were running blast furnaces worked by double-acting piston bellows, pouring liquid cast iron and stirring it into steel; by the sixth century the metallurgist Qiwu Huaiwen had written down a way of making steel by melting cast iron over wrought; and right through the Song, China had the most advanced iron industry in the world, centuries ahead of Europe.13 You would think that would make it the world’s knife shop, and it didn’t, partly because a Chinese kitchen ran on one tool, the cleaver, made by the local smith for the local market,14 and mostly because the law wouldn’t let it.

The Qing code filed knives where you’d file anything that might end up in a pirate’s hand: under military law, in the statutes on frontier passes and harbours, which promised a hundred strokes of the heavy bamboo for running iron goods out of the country and strangulation for exporting weapons.12

Leaving Canton: one cast-iron wok and one axe per crewman, and anything more went to the customs house.15 放行 means “cleared”; 不准, “not permitted.”

When Western ships were later confined to Canton, a departing crew could take exactly that, a wok and an axe a man, and anything more went to the customs house;15 so silk, tea and porcelain went out by the shipload, and the cleaver stayed home.

What was the Canton System?

From 1757 to 1842 the Qing let Western merchants trade at one port only, Guangzhou, which the British called Canton, and only through a small group of licensed Chinese merchant houses, the Cohong; foreigners weren’t allowed inland, so what left China was whatever those houses, and the customs house, let out.16

Meanwhile the trade in good blades ran the other way: from 1404 into the middle of the 1500s, Ming China imported more than a hundred thousand Japanese swords through the official tally trade,17 which is worth remembering for later, when the arrow turns round.

What was the tally trade?

The Ming only traded with Japan through official missions, and each Japanese ship had to carry half of a numbered tally certificate that matched the half kept in China; no matching tally, no trade.18

The year is 1800, and the world’s foremost City of Knives is

3Sheffield

The first city to hold the title of the world’s foremost City of Knives was an English one, and it won it on the strength of better steel.

In the 1740s Benjamin Huntsman worked out, near Sheffield, how to melt steel in clay crucibles, which gave the town’s cutlers an even, slag-free metal that took an edge and held it better than anything their rivals could forge; the Company of Cutlers in Hallamshire, chartered by Parliament back in 1624, policed the marks, and for the better part of two centuries “Sheffield” on a blade was about the closest thing the world had to a guarantee.19

What’s crucible steel, and who were the Cutlers?

Steel melted in sealed clay pots: the slag floats off and the carbon evens out, so you get one uniform, clean steel instead of the streaky stuff that came out of a forge. The Company of Cutlers in Hallamshire was Sheffield’s trade guild, chartered by Act of Parliament in 1624; it registered makers’ marks and enforced standards, so a mark on a blade meant something.19

That’s why the belt in the corner now says Sheffield, for now. Before it moves, though, it’s worth knowing what “better steel” actually means.

PLATE 1Carbon

More carbon: a harder edge, but a more brittle one, too.

Steel is iron with a little carbon in it, and in a knife that little carbon is most of the story; pick any of four real kitchen steels, from a cheap cleaver steel to a Japanese carbon steel, and drop the same thin edge onto a bone to see what each one does when it meets something hard.202122

What’s HRC?

Rockwell C hardness: you press a diamond cone into the steel under a set load and measure how far it sinks; kitchen knives run from about 52, soft and forgiving, to about 66, very hard and brittle.23

4Made in Germany

Britain tried to shame German knives with a label; the label ended up selling them.

In 1876 Germany’s own commissioner at the Philadelphia exhibition, Franz Reuleaux, wrote home that his country’s goods had earned two words, billig und schlecht, cheap and bad, and, worse, that they deserved them: German industry had been competing on price, he said, while “the other industrial nations have, by majority, gone the way of competition through quality,” der Konkurrenz durch Qualität;25 but Solingen’s makers had taken to drop-forging and die-stamping while Sheffield still ran on a warren of small outworkers’ shops, and the German knives came in at a fifth to two-fifths cheaper.26

What was the Philadelphia exhibition?

The Centennial International Exhibition of 1876, the first official world’s fair held in the United States, staged in Philadelphia for the hundredth anniversary of the Declaration of Independence; nearly ten million visits were recorded, and every industrial nation sent its best work, which is how a German official came to be standing in a hall full of his country’s exhibits deciding they were cheap and bad.27

Sheffield’s answer was the Merchandise Marks Act of 1887, which made foreign goods say where they came from so that British shoppers would know what they were buying; they did know, as it turned out, and within twenty-odd years “Made in Germany” had become a mark people went looking for.28

Then, in 1890, the McKinley Tariff all but shut Sheffield out of America, its biggest market,29 and the long slide in the chart began.

What was the McKinley Tariff?

The Tariff Act of 1890, written by an Ohio congressman named William McKinley, who went on to be the 25th president: it pushed average American import duties up to around 50% to protect home industry, hit imported cutlery hard, and, as a side effect that matters later in this story, required every import to be marked with its country of origin.30

And the “cheap and bad” verdict? That’s where the most German thing in this whole story happens: instead of sulking, the country took Reuleaux’s insult as a to-do list. It couldn’t beat Britain on sheer volume, so it went after quality, pouring money into technical universities, the Technische Hochschulen, and research institutes, into chemistry at BASF, Bayer and Hoechst, optics at Zeiss and Leitz, and electrical engineering, so that by 1914 “Made in Germany” was a compliment;31 and that bet on engineering education is, more or less, what the German economy has run on ever since.

The belt, you’ll notice, is packing its bags.

The year is 1900, and the world’s foremost City of Knives is

5Solingen

Solingen made swords for centuries before it made kitchen knives, and its knife companies, when they tell their own history, would rather you started in 1731 and skipped a couple of decades in the middle.

The Dukes of Berg chartered its blade guilds one trade at a time: the hardeners and grinders in 1401, the assemblers in 1412 and 1420, the bladesmiths in 1472; and when guns made swords less interesting, the same shops went over to scissors, pocketknives and kitchen knives, which is how you get the Zwilling twins entered in the cutlers’ roll in 1731 and Wüsthof opening its doors in 1814.32

The classic Solingen chef’s knife is drop-forged, heavy, and finished with a bolster, the thick collar of steel where blade meets handle; it’s a lovely thing to hold, and it has one flaw that only shows up years later, which the next plate will show you faster than you’d like.

PLATE 2The bolster

A full bolster: solid in the hand, but the stone can’t reach it, and the edge wears away from under it.

Drag through the years of sharpening: every session takes a little steel off the edge, but none off the bolster, and eventually the heel no longer touches the board at all, which knife people call “bolster hang.”33

Then there’s the law that made the name itself property. On July 25, 1938, Hitler’s government enacted the Act for the Protection of the Name Solingen, printed in the Reich Law Gazette, after years of lobbying by the town’s chamber of commerce and its cutlery makers; it suited the regime nicely: it tidied small workshops into cartels, it protected an export that earned foreign currency for rearmament, and it wrote “German quality work” into law.34

What was the Reich Law Gazette?

The Reichsgesetzblatt, the official journal in which German laws were published from 1871 to 1945; a law wasn’t in force until it was printed there, so the Solingen law has a page number, I p. 953, like any other.3534

And the factories, of course, went to war: Henckels, Wüsthof, Böker and Eickhorn, among others, made bayonets, military knives and the dress daggers of the SA, the SS and the rest of the Nazi apparatus,36 which is the part the company histories tend to skip, preferring the eighteenth-century craftsmen and the postwar design classics.37

Forced labor

Solingen ran short of workers once its men were drafted, and the gap was filled with people taken from occupied Europe. The city had fewer than 1,000 foreign forced workers at the end of 1941 and more than 8,000 by December 1943; on November 1, 1944 there were 10,800 registered, about 6,800 men and 4,000 women, along with some 2,000 prisoners of war.38

Henckels alone held more than 700 civilian forced laborers from the Soviet Union and Poland, and 36 prisoners of war, in three camps; when the RAF bombed the city on November 4 and 5, 1944, at least 150 forced laborers were killed, many of them kept in wooden barracks while German workers went to the shelters.39

The 1938 law outlived the regime that passed it: rewritten as the Solingen decree of December 16, 1994, in force from the first day of 1995, it still says that the word “Solingen” can only go on a blade whose essential stages were all carried out in Solingen or neighbouring Haan,40 which is, to be fair, exactly the kind of promise a knife buyer would want.

Under the McKinley Tariff of 1890, Japanese porcelain was stamped “Nippon” from 1891; US Customs decided that wasn’t English and required “Japan” from September 1, 1921; from 1947 to 1952, exports said “Made in Occupied Japan.”41

6Nippon → Japan → Occupied Japan

Japan’s first famous mark was a customs requirement, and for decades it meant “cheap.”

The plate in your grandmother’s china cabinet tells the story on its underside: the stamps changed because American customs law changed, not because Japan did, and for most of that time the word on the back was a warning to bargain hunters rather than a boast.41

Knives went through the same mill, and they started from swords. On March 28, 1876, the Meiji government banned the carrying of swords in public, and the swordsmiths of Seki, who had been making blades since the Middle Ages, went over to pocket knives, farm tools and Western-style cutlery to stay alive.42

Then came the war, and the swords came back: from about 1940 the army’s inspectors had a small 関 stamped into the tang of every sword Seki made from industrial steel by machine, to keep it apart from the traditionally forged ones, while the town’s registered swordsmiths went from one in 1938 to 232 by September 1944.43 A good many of the “samurai swords” that came home in duffel bags were, as it turns out, factory steel with a stamp to say so, and Japan still won’t register them as art swords.43

After the surrender the occupation banned edged weapons outright; Seki, which nobody had bothered to bomb, turned its presses to pocket knives, razors and kitchen knives, first for the American PX and then for export,44 and in 1957 Japan passed an Export Inspection Law that simply forbade shipping designated goods, cutlery among them, that failed government tests.45

The knives followed the cameras: MAC, founded in Seki in 1964, made thin stamped chef’s knives that Western chefs at first dismissed as flimsy; Global, designed by Komin Yamada for Yoshikin in Niigata, arrived in 1985; and in 2000 Anthony Bourdain wrote, in Kitchen Confidential, that “most of the professionals I know have for years been retiring their Wusthofs and replacing them with the lightweight, easy-to-sharpen and relatively inexpensive vanadium steel Global knives, very good Japanese products that have—in addition to their many other fine qualities—the added attraction of looking really cool.”4647 Which brings us to the most important thing about a Japanese knife, which is the angle of its edge.

PLATE 3Edge angle and bevel

Thinner: it cuts easier, but it breaks easier, too, which is the whole German–Japanese argument in one slider.

Drag the angle and the edge is redrawn at that angle, while the bar shows the trend in how long it stays sharp: in hundreds of cutting-machine tests, every extra degree cost a bit of edge life, and the cost grows the sharper you go; at the same 30-degree edge, the best and worst steels differed nearly fivefold, so the steel matters too, but as the man who ran the tests puts it, edge angle and the sharpening “have a greater effect on performance than steel choice”; German factories grind somewhere around 30 to 40 degrees in total, Japanese ones around 24 to 30.48

Then there’s the bevel: most knives are ground on both sides, but the traditional Japanese fish and vegetable knives are ground on one side only, with a hollow back, so they steer toward the flat side as they cut, which a sushi chef corrects for without thinking and a home cook discovers the hard way.49

The year is 2000, and the world’s foremost City of Knives depends on how heavy you like your knife.

The title has split in two, the way boxing titles do: the heavyweight belt stays in Solingen, with its 220-to-280-gram forged chef’s knives, and the lightweight belt goes to Seki, Japan’s biggest blade town, where the thinnest gyutos weigh as little as 120 to 160 grams.50 Check the corner: the belt has just divided.

7Meanwhile, back in Yangjiang

While the world argued about German and Japanese edges, a Yangjiang smith had already put both on one blade.

Cantonese cooks used to keep two cleavers, a thin one for slicing and a thick one for bone, until He Quanli, in the 1920s, ground the front two-thirds of a single cleaver thin and left the back third thick, and called it the 文武刀, wenwudao, the “civil-military knife”; it sold for four hao of silver and ended up in restaurant kitchens from Guangzhou to Hong Kong.51

A decade later Liang Jifu, 梁季芙, a jeweller by training, started making pocketknives out of spring steel salvaged from vehicles and railway scrap, with buffalo-horn handles and his name stamped on every blade;52 Yangjiang had no blast furnaces, so it did what poor knife towns everywhere do, and made a little good steel go a long way.

PLATE 5The sandwich

Yangjiang’s poor man’s workaround and Japan’s prized craft: slide one over the other and see how different they are.

The trick was to forge-weld a thin core of hard steel between two cheeks of soft iron, 夹钢, xiagang, so the edge is hard and the body forgiving; Japanese smiths do the same thing with hard hagane between soft jigane, and call it san-mai.53

8The ghost years

For a generation, the best way to sell a Yangjiang knife was to put someone else’s name on it.

Most of what the city makes is made to order for brands abroad: by one count about seventy percent to the customer’s own designs, another quarter from the factory’s catalogue under the customer’s name, and only about five percent under a Yangjiang brand at all.57

And then there’s the internet’s own contribution: a whole genre of online knife brands with Japanese-sounding names, samurai on the box and sword lore in the copy, selling knives made in Yangjiang;58 which completes the reversal nicely, since five hundred years ago it was China importing real Japanese swords by the hundred thousand, and now it’s China exporting the “Japanese” knives.17

9The comeback

China’s first move was the one Japan made a century earlier: learn the other side’s steel, and make it at home.

10Cr15CoMoV is, near enough, the Japanese stainless VG-10 with a Chinese name: the same carbon, chromium, molybdenum, vanadium and cobalt, each inside VG-10’s own specified range;59 and the same goes down the list, with 5Cr15MoV standing in for the German X50CrMoV15, and so on.59

The catch, as every knife nerd will tell you at length, is that the name on the steel tells you much less than what was done to it in the furnace.60

PLATE 6Heat treatment

Same steel, two furnaces: the recipe matters more than the name on the bar.

Run the same bar of steel through a careful heat treatment, with a vacuum furnace, a deep freeze and two tempers, and through a rushed one, and you get two different knives; each point of hardness the furnace fails to deliver costs a little edge life, and a rushed job leaves soft spots in the steel’s structure on top of that.60

What Yangjiang has that nobody else has is everything else: steel slitting, blanking and forging, heat treatment, grinding, handles and packing, all within fifteen or thirty kilometres of each other, fed by a local mill that runs a 400,000-ton-a-year line just for cutlery strip;61 the fair’s organisers like to call it a “one-hour supply-chain circle,” which is a brochure line, but not much of an exaggeration. At Jinhui, which now owns the 1651 Beijing brand Wangmazi, one automatic stamping line turns out 120,000 pieces a day, which the company reckons is the work of 24 skilled workers on ten-hour shifts;62 and the old famous names, Zhang Xiaoquan from Hangzhou and Wangmazi from Beijing, have moved production down to Yangjiang to be near it all.63

None of which guarantees a good knife, as Zhang Xiaoquan found out in July 2022, when one of its cleavers snapped in half while a customer was smashing garlic with it: the company pointed out that 60,000 had been sold with eleven complaints, and the internet pointed out that smashing garlic is what a Chinese cleaver is for.64

PLATE 7The garlic smash

Hard enough to hold an edge, tough enough to survive a slap: a cleaver has to be both.

Set the hardness and bring the flat of the blade down on a clove: a harder blade keeps its edge longer, but push a thin one too far without the toughness to match and the slap that should crush the garlic cracks the steel instead. (A drawing of the trade-off, not a test of any real knife.)6421

By volume the argument is over: in 2024 China exported $1.66 billion worth of knives, more than half the world’s total, against $292 million from Germany and $167 million from Japan;3 so the year is 2024, and by sheer tonnage the world’s foremost City of Knives is Yangjiang, which earns it a third belt, even if the two prestige belts haven’t moved. America, the biggest customer, now adds about 24.6% in duties, plus 0.8 cents, to every Chinese kitchen knife it lets in.365

What are the Section 301 tariffs?

Extra duties the US has put on Chinese goods since 2018, under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974; for kitchen knives they now stack up as 7.5% plus a 12.5% “forced-labor” duty on top of the ordinary 4.6%.6665

10Does the country matter?

Not really: what matters is the knife, and the knife is decided by five things, none of which is a flag.

Every one of them is decided by the maker’s machines and the maker’s care: how thin they grind behind the edge, how well they control the furnace, how carefully they finish; none of it is unique to any country, which is why the tests keep embarrassing the expensive names.67

America’s Test Kitchen has picked a cheap stamped Victorinox over forged German knives costing several times as much, year after year, because it’s thin and light;68 and Cangshan, a California brand with its own factories in Yangjiang, reported that its blades in Swedish 14C28N cut 850 millimetres of test card before dulling, against 601 for its own blades in the German standard steel.69

The belt, accordingly, has been vacated.

11What’s stamped on yours?

Now go and look at your own knife, because the answer is stamped on the blade, or, tellingly, it isn’t.

Some of these words are promises with a law behind them, and some are carefully chosen to sound like one: “Solingen” and “Sheffield” are protected, “Made in Japan” means the knife was actually made there, and “German steel,” “Japanese steel core” and “Designed in Germany” tell you where the metal or the drawing came from and nothing at all about where the knife was made.704071

And if it says “Made in China,” well: now you know where that is, and roughly what goes on there, and that the only thing it tells you about the knife is which side of the world it was finished on.
